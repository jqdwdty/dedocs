## Design Context

### Users
Dedocs is for technical builders: AI-tooling engineers, open-source maintainers, document pipeline developers, and technically fluent product people who need to inspect, version, and transform `.docx` documents as source. They are usually evaluating whether the format is real, exact, and practical before adopting it.

Their core job is to understand the model quickly:
- how dedocs maps a `.docx` package into one file
- what fidelity it guarantees
- what the semantic editing layer actually does
- whether the examples and proof artifacts are trustworthy

### Brand Personality
Exact. Lucid. Uncompromising.

The interface should feel like a carefully edited technical field manual, not a generic developer landing page and not a glossy SaaS dashboard. It should project confidence, seriousness, and precision without becoming cold or lifeless.

### Aesthetic Direction
Light-first, editorial, and toolmaker-oriented.

Use warm paper-like surfaces, tinted neutrals, and a restrained oxidized teal plus rust accent. Typography should feel deliberate and literate: a refined serif for major statements and a sober sans for body/interface text. Code should feel integrated into the page, not bolted on as decorative “developer vibes.”

Anti-references:
- generic SaaS gradients and dashboard cards
- glassmorphism and neon dark-mode aesthetics
- purple-blue AI branding clichés
- hero metric templates and nested card grids

### Design Principles
1. Make fidelity tangible immediately. Visitors should understand package-exactness in the first screen.
2. Show the system, not just the slogan. Core, guides, transforms, and proof artifacts should stay visible throughout the page.
3. Treat the page like a dossier. Use asymmetry, rules, labels, and contrast to make it feel inspectable and exact.
4. Keep every visual decision purposeful. Motion should reveal structure, not decorate emptiness.
5. Prefer clarity over hype. The site should earn trust by being concrete, inspectable, and static.
6. Borrow the explanatory density of a serious product manual: use cases, quick start, concrete commands, and the actual workflow should be obvious without reading the README first.
